## Terminal and Instrumental goals

Watching [Robert Miles' video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZeecOKBus3Q&pp=ygUXaW5zdHJ1bWVuYWwgY29udmVyZ2VuY2U%3D) on goal-seeking behavior brought this new idea and subsequent understanding of terminal and instrumental goals to my mind. Most of us understand that an agent is nothing but an autonomous software system powered by an LLM with a specific goal and specific actions it can take to achieve them. 

But, I always had this preconceived notion that for an intelligent system to do something that would be considered harmful to humans, it had to actually develop harmful intentions. But in reality that is actually the case very few times, and instrumental convergence is actually one idea that proves so.

Essentially, like humans, agents have goals, and they take actions to achieve these goals. Now, unlike humans, agents will do anything in their power to achieve their goals, and so certain steps along the way (instrumental goals) such as accumulating certain resources, goal preservation, and not being shut down are steps an AI would take to accomplish these goals. 

Now there are two things that cause issues here. The first is that AI doesn't have a "moral compass" which (hopefully) most humans do. This means that an agent's terminal goals can be anything. A human may find dedicating their life to counting grains of sand, or making as many paper clips as possible pointless, but general philosophy tells us that terminal goals inherently cannot be deemed "stupid," since there's nothing to reference them to. If you want something just to want it, that preference is fundamental. This is part of the orthogonality thesis. 

So the issue is that we can give an AI agent any goal, and most likely it will seek out accumulating certain resources, goal preservation, and not being shut down are steps as instrumental goals, and since these tasks are essential to accomplish almost any terminal goal, we can predict that these actions are inevitable, also known as instrumental convergence. 

Instrumental convergence can cause issues since things like accumulating resources can mean damaging human ecosystems, and self-preservation can mean infiltrating key technological infrastructure, all things that don't align with the human moral compass, but an agent may deem necessary for accomplishing whatever goal it's given. 


## Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier AI Models

A [METR article](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) describes a potential method for calculating improvements in AI by analyzing the frequency of which AI models can accurately complete tasks that take humans increasingly greater amounts of time. 

The article claims that the 50% task completion time horizon has historically doubled every 7 months with recent reports showing a doubling time of just 3 months. This is incredible pace, and completely blows away Moore's law. I think it shows just how fast the growth rate of AI has been recently, and also presents a more comprehensive and real-world-adapted way of measuring model performance—especially on programming tasks. 

However, there are a few questions I have, specifically on the significance of these findings:
1. How is the difficulty of the task standardized
2. How is the skill level of the programmer creating the benchmark determined and clustered accurately
3. How is it determined if a task is completed successfully or not

The fact that METR claims that measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable supports these question I think, since the longer a task the more opportunities there are for slight variations in the human programmer's skill level to largely affect the time it takes them to complete the task.

## Takeaways

I think that the seemingly strong rationale for AI-facilitated harm to human society via instrumental convergence, as well as the blistering speed at which AI claims to be improving provide a compelling case to study AI safety and alignment.

Everyone has an opinion on whether AI will take over the world or just "burst" it's own bubble, but only few of these discussions include substantial rationale or reasoning as to why either would happen. Furthermore, it may not be as simple as speculating on this dichotomy, but rather what we can do to ensure the former won't happen.

That's why I think this field is so interesting: it gives us the opportunity to take control of our future, understand the inner-workings of this revolutionary technology, and play our own part in preventing an AI armageddon.
